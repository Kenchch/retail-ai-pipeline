# Frequently Bought Together — user guide

**For:** category managers, merchandisers, online trading, marketing
**Reading time:** 5 minutes

---

## What it is

For any product, this shows the products most often bought in the same basket,
ranked by how much stronger that pairing is than chance. It is built from
522,566 real transaction lines across 16,782 baskets and refreshes overnight.

## What it is not

It tells you **what customers did**. Not what they will do, and not why. Two
products can appear together because they are genuinely complementary, because
they sat next to each other on a page, or because they were in the same
promotion. You still bring the judgement — this makes sure you are judging the
right candidates.

## Reading a row

| Product | Recommended | Baskets | Confidence | Lift |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CHILDS GARDEN SPADE PINK | CHILDS GARDEN SPADE BLUE | 40 | 0.66 | 234 |

**Baskets — 40.** How many baskets contained both. This is your sample size. A
lift of 300 on 8 baskets is noise; the same lift on 200 is a finding. **Read
this first, every time.**

**Confidence — 0.66.** Of baskets with the pink spade, 66% also had the blue.

**Lift — 234.** The pairing is 234× more likely than if the two were unrelated.
This is the ranking column.

### How to read lift

| Lift | Means |
|---|---|
| ~1 | No relationship — ignore |
| 2–5 | Real but mild. Fine for a "you might also like" slot |
| 10+ | Strong — usually a genuine complement |
| 100+ | A colour/size variant or two halves of one purchase occasion |

Confidence alone is misleading, which is why it is not the ranking column: a
product in a third of all baskets shows high confidence against everything.
Lift corrects for how often the product shows up anyway.

---

## The `method` column — read this one

**`co_purchase`** — built from what people actually bought together. The real
signal. Trust it in proportion to the basket count. Covers about **30%** of the
catalogue.

**`content_tfidf`** — built from the product *description text*, not behaviour.
You will see it on newer and slow-moving lines. It is a reasonable starting
point ("these are described similarly") but it is **not evidence that anyone
ever bought them together**.

---

## Three things it will get wrong

**It will recommend the free gift.** A promotional item that went in every
basket for a fortnight pairs with everything from that period.

**It cannot see the reason.** Bunting and paper plates co-occur because both are
party purchases, not because bunting drives plate demand. Placing them together
is sensible; discounting bunting to lift plates is not.

**It is blind to what you did not stock.** A product out of stock for six weeks
builds its pairings from the weeks it was available, and looks less connected
than it is.

---

## What it is good for

**Adjacency and planogram decisions** — filter to your category, sort by lift,
look for pairs in different aisles. That gap is the opportunity.

**Bundle candidates** — high confidence *and* high basket count means people are
already buying the bundle themselves. Formalising an existing behaviour is a far
easier sell than creating a new one.

**Online "customers also bought"** — top 3 by lift, filtered to in-stock lines.

**Long-tail discovery** — the thing nobody has the head-space to do manually
across 3,803 products.

## FAQ

**"Is it just guessing?"** No. It counts real baskets. Nothing is generated by a
language model — it is arithmetic over your own transaction history, and every
number traces back to it.

**"Why does it recommend something silly?"** In order of likelihood: low basket
count; it is a `content_tfidf` row; or the two products were in the same
promotion. If none of those explain it, tell us — that is a real finding.

**"Can it tell me what a specific customer will buy?"** Not in this release, and
not by accident — a quarter of transactions have no customer id, so a
customer-level model would be built on the loyalty-card population and quietly
generalised to everyone.

**"Will the AI change prices or promotions?"** No. Nothing writes to a trading
system. Every recommendation is a suggestion a person accepts or rejects.

**"Why do my numbers differ from my colleague's?"** Almost always refresh
timing — the data refreshes overnight, so a report left open from yesterday
shows yesterday. If they still differ after a refresh, that is a real defect and
we want to hear about it.

**"It has barely changed since last month. Is it broken?"** Probably not.
Co-purchase patterns over a full trading history are stable by design — fast
movement would mean the model was chasing noise. New and seasonal lines are
where to expect change.
